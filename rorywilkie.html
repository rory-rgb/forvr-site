I make AI visuals, and build systems to make them repeatable.

I extract a brand's visual identity and DNA, then build repeatable systems that align with the brand and fit its brand kit, so every asset is immediately recognisable as that brand.

Every shoot starts with that identity locked, and anything that has to stay the same, a product, a character, a location, is built once as a reference sheet before a single frame exists. That is what makes the work repeatable, and what makes it handable to someone else.

Statue of a Fool

Scope
Direction, garment, campaign

17 · 3:4

A collection built end to end, from written direction through to a finished campaign. It starts as a written codex rather than a prompt, with the collection's visual identity locked before a single frame exists, which gives everything made afterwards something to be judged against.

It swings between two poles on purpose. Direct on-camera flash, hard shadow and wet pavement at one end. Chiaroscuro, marble and monumental stillness at the other. Both have to be present or the whole thing reads as a clean render instead of a real campaign. The palette is sampled off the finished garments and the streets they were shot in, not picked from swatches.

Forvr Collection

Scope

Built in

Frames
7 · 4:5

The first brand I built, and the hardest product-consistency test in the portfolio. One garment, a black tracksuit with contrast blue stitching, carried across chain link, a corrugated shutter, bare concrete, a wet underpass and a stairwell. Different light, different surfaces, different distances, the same piece reading correctly in all of it.

The colour is what holds the set together rather than the styling: the blue in the stitching picks up the shutter, the underpass fluorescents and the enamel on the signet ring.

It was built with a Weavy workflow that connects the moodboard through to prompt concatenators, so the prompt for every frame is assembled inside the graph rather than written one at a time. Ellyette runs on the same workflow.

03StillsConcept shoot

Ellyette

4 frames · 4:5 · 2026
01 / 04View set →
Type
Concept shoot, streetwear
Built in
Weavy, Nano Banana Pro
Frames
4 · 4:5

Ellyette's line is "from the office to the field", and they had only ever photographed the office. So this set stays in a 1970s office after hours and the field never arrives: rosewood panelling, drop-ceiling fluorescents, a rail of their own pieces, and one man with a job he is ambivalent about. Every frame is an event rather than a pose. He takes the hoodie off the rail, the desk phone interrupts him, he waits for someone who has not come back, and he leaves.

It was built with the same Weavy workflow as the Forvr Collection. The moodboard is analysed inside the graph, a constants node and a shot brief are joined by prompt concatenators into one prompt per frame, and a checker node reads them before anything renders. Every render gets the same three references in the same order: the character sheet, the hoodie and the room. The hoodie is never described in words, because describing a garment the model can already see makes it redraw it.

04StillsConcept set

Strano

4 frames · 4:5 · 2026
01 / 04View set →
Type
Concept set, belts and jewellery
Method
One edit pass per frame
Frames
4 · 4:5

Strano is an Italian belt and jewellery brand, and the set puts their belts on the street in hard sun: a London bus stop, a rooftop, a pink wall, and a close macro of two belts stacked on denim.

The belts are real products. Each one goes into the edit as a photograph and is never described in the prompt, because words make the model rebuild the emboss, the studs and the buckle, and that is exactly where they drift. The concept frames were approved first, then restyled to carry the belts in a single edit pass, never two deep.

05FilmOwn film

Seaforth

24 shots · 16:9 · sound on
Type
Own film
Built
Generated to match a real plate
Post
Directed, cut and sound-designed in-house

A perfume, a tube carriage, and the thirty seconds a smell can take off you. A man catches something he recognises, and what follows is not a flashback so much as a slip: a bedroom in morning light, a pub, a corridor, a door.

The carriage, the seat, the position and the light are the real thing rather than invented, and everything generated downstream was built to match them. The sound does most of the work at the hinge: the platform pre-laps the cut, so you are already somewhere else before the picture agrees.

06FilmSpec film

Protect, Touchdown

23s · 16:9 · sound on
Type
Spec film
Built from
A character sheet, a product sheet, one location plate
Wardrobe
Protect's Touchdown crewneck and sweat shorts

A London kid in a full American baseball kit, sat in a knackered armchair on a black sand beach, throwing a ball up and catching it. He throws one too hard and loses it in the sun. He waits, shrugs and walks off, and the ball lands beside the empty chair after he has gone.

The joke is Protect's own, lifted from their product copy, which wonders whether there is such a thing as a baseball college. The kit is theirs too, built as a product sheet so the script lettering holds in every shot. The kid, the chair and the beach were each built once and locked, and the ten shots come out of two generations split where he stands up, so the film's own act break hides the seam.

07FilmMotion

Relay & Blowup

Two films · 16:9 · sound on

Relay is the technical one. Three tracksuit looks, three rooms of one London flat, and a single horizontal camera move through the whole thing that never cuts away to reset. Each person gets six beats, then the dividing wall crosses the lens and fills it, and that is the hand-off into the next room. Room brightness runs dark warm, bright, dark cool, so the film has a shape before the garments do any work at all. It is cut to loop.

How I work

The part that transfers
  1. 01

    Visual identity and DNA, locked first

    Before a frame exists, the shoot's visual identity and brand DNA are extracted and locked. Every frame is made against that, so a whole set is recognisably one shoot rather than a run of separate generations.

  2. 02

    Sheets for anything that has to hold

    Products, characters and locations that must not drift are built once as reference sheets: a multi-angle product sheet, a six-panel character sheet with three full-length views and three face angles, an empty location plate. Every generation pulls from the sheets, never from a description.

  3. 03

    The reference carries the product

    A garment or a belt goes in as a photograph and never gets described in words. The reference already holds the colour, the print and the hardware, and describing it only makes the model redraw it.

  4. 04

    Real plates where they exist

    Where something was actually photographed, the generated material is built to match it rather than replace it. Generative work sits under photography and post, not over the top of them.

  5. 05

    Written down, so it transfers

    Producing weekly to a deadline is what forces a process out of your head and onto paper. Anything held together by memory falls over by the third week. The Weavy workflows work the same way: swap the moodboard, the product and the brief, and the same graph runs the next brand.
